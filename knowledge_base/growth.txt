Based on the provided World Health Organization (WHO) guideline document, “Improving Early Childhood Development: WHO Guideline”, here are the answers to your questions:  
1. Growth Milestones
The document defines Early Childhood Development (ECD) as the cognitive, physical, language, temperament, socioemotional, and motor development of children from conception up to 8 years of age.  
Critical Period: The first 3 years (and early childhood overall) represent a critical window of rapid brain development where children are most responsive to nurturing care.  
Key Developmental Domains Evaluated:
Cognitive & Language Development: Encompasses thinking, reasoning, learning, and both receptive and expressive communication skills.  
Motor Development: Includes fine motor skills (hand-eye coordination, manipulating objects) and gross motor skills.  
Socioemotional & Behavioral Development: Covers attachment, social interactions, emotional expression, and behavior management.  
2. Height and Weight
In the guideline, physical growth outcomes are tracked using standardized WHO Z-scores:  
HAZ (Height-for-Age Z-score): Measures linear growth; low HAZ indicates stunting.  
WAZ (Weight-for-Age Z-score): Measures overall body weight relative to age; low WAZ indicates underweight.  
WHZ (Weight-for-Height/Length Z-score): Measures acute weight relative to height; low WHZ indicates wasting.  
Key Findings:
Combined caregiving and nutrition interventions significantly improved WHZ in children.
Standalone responsive caregiving interventions showed a moderate positive effect on improving HAZ in specific trial contexts.
3. BMI
Definition: Body Mass Index (BMI) for age is used as an outcome measure for assessing child nutritional status, growth, and over-nutrition/obesity.  
Document Evidence: Psychosocial maternal mental health interventions were evaluated against child BMI-for-age, though single-component studies showed no statistically significant direct change on child BMI alone.
4. Healthy Development
The document grounds healthy child development in the concept of Nurturing Care. Nurturing care requires a stable environment that provides:  
Good Health  
Adequate Nutrition  
Security and Safety (protection from adversity, maltreatment, and harsh punishment)  
Responsive Caregiving (prompt, sensitive, consistent, and appropriate responses to a child’s cues)  
Opportunities for Early Learning (play, exploration, and positive interactions)  
5. Nutrition for Growth
Recommendation: WHO strongly recommends that support for responsive caregiving and early learning must be integrated with optimal nutrition interventions.
Key Insight: Nutrition interventions alone (such as supplementation or food distribution) are not sufficient to maximize a child's cognitive and motor development unless combined with responsive caregiving and early learning opportunities.
Responsive Feeding: Feeding should be interactive—caregivers must be attuned to the child's hunger and fullness cues rather than using forceful feeding.
6. Sleep and Growth
Context: The document highlights sleep pattern changes as a critical indicator in caregiver mental health (e.g., maternal depression).  
Parenting Practices: Establishing daily family routines—which include consistent sleep and bed routines—is emphasized as a key positive behavior management strategy to support healthy socioemotional and physical child development.
7. Physical Activity
Play & Exploration: Children acquire, consolidate, and refine motor and cognitive skills through playful physical interactions with people and objects.  
Integrated Care: Combining play-based activities and motor stimulation alongside routine health and nutrition services led to statistically significant improvements in both fine and gross motor development.
8. Growth Monitoring
Growth monitoring should extend beyond simply taking anthropometric measurements (height and weight):
Integrated Services: Growth tracking should occur alongside developmental monitoring (cognitive, motor, and language checks) during routine health clinic visits, immunizations, and home visits.
Targeting High-Risk Groups: Monitoring is especially critical for vulnerable populations, such as undernourished or stunted children, who show the largest developmental gains when given targeted caregiving interventions.
9. When to Consult a Doctor
Caregivers and health services should seek medical evaluation or clinical intervention if:
Growth Faltering / Malnutrition: A child exhibits stunting, severe underweight, or wasting (low HAZ, WAZ, or WHZ).
Developmental Delays: A child shows signs of significant motor, cognitive, or language delays.  
Caregiver Mental Health Crises: Mothers/caregivers experience severe depressive symptoms, anxiety, or emotional distress during pregnancy or early childhood, as maternal mental health directly impacts child care and health outcomes.
Child Maltreatment or Illness: High incidence of acute illnesses (e.g., severe diarrhea, acute respiratory infection) or suspected neglect/abuse.  
10. Fun Growth Tip
Interactive Play & "Talk, Sing, Play" Daily:
You don't need expensive toys to boost your child's growth and brain development! Every routine moment—like feeding or bath time—is an opportunity for early learning. Talk to your baby, imitate their sounds, read books together, and respond playfully to their cues. Simple, affectionate interactions literally build your child's brain structure!
