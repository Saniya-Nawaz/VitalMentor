1. Why is Water Important?
•	Growth & Development: Water supports digestion, nutrient transport, temperature regulation, and healthy kidney function during periods of rapid growth.
•	Higher Vulnerability: Children are more susceptible to dehydration than adults because they have a higher body-surface-area-to-mass ratio, lose fluid faster through the skin, and may not reliably recognize or communicate thirst.
•	Cognitive & Physical Performance: Even mild dehydration in children is associated with reduced concentration, short-term memory, alertness, and physical endurance during play or sport.
2. Daily Water Requirement
•	0–6 Months: No plain water needed. Breast milk or formula supplies all fluid requirements (equivalent to roughly 100–190 mL/kg/day, EFSA).
•	6–12 Months: Approximately 800–1,000 mL/day total water, mostly from breast milk/formula, with small sips of plain water introduced alongside solid foods (EFSA).
•	1–3 Years: About 1.1–1.3 L/day total fluids (roughly 4 cups), including water, milk, and food moisture (EFSA/IOM).
•	4–8 Years: About 1.6–1.7 L/day total fluids (roughly 5 cups) (EFSA/IOM).
•	9–13 Years: About 1.9–2.1 L/day for girls and 2.1–2.4 L/day for boys (roughly 7–8 cups) (EFSA/IOM).
Note: these are total fluid intake figures — they include water, milk, and other beverages, plus roughly 20% contributed by food moisture — not plain drinking water alone.
3. Signs of Dehydration
•	Infants: Fewer than 6 wet diapers per day, no tears when crying, sunken soft spot (fontanelle), and unusual drowsiness or irritability.
•	Mild–Moderate: Dry mouth or lips, thirst, dark yellow urine, headache, and reduced activity or attention span.
•	Severe (Emergency): Sunken eyes, rapid heartbeat, rapid breathing, extreme lethargy, or confusion — seek medical care immediately.
4. Benefits of Drinking Water
•	Cognitive Function: Supports concentration, memory, and mood, particularly important for school performance.
•	Physical Performance: Maintains energy and endurance during play, sport, and hot weather.
•	Digestive Health: Helps prevent constipation and supports healthy kidney function.
5. Best Drinks
•	Plain Water: The best primary source of fluid for children over 6 months.
•	Milk: An appropriate source of fluid and nutrients in age-appropriate amounts.
•	Water-Rich Foods: Fruits and vegetables (melon, cucumber, oranges) contribute roughly 20% of a child's daily fluid needs (AAP).
•	Oral Rehydration Solutions (ORS): The recommended choice for fluid replacement during diarrheal illness or vomiting.
6. Drinks to Avoid
•	No Water Before 6 Months: Plain water is not recommended for infants under 6 months, as it can displace essential nutrition and, in rare cases, cause water intoxication.
•	Limit Fruit Juice: AAP recommends no juice under age 1; no more than about 4 oz/day for ages 1–3 and 4–6 oz/day for ages 4–6.
•	Sugar-Sweetened & Caffeinated Beverages: Sodas, energy drinks, and sports drinks should be limited or avoided, as they add excess sugar without meaningful hydration benefit for most children.
7. Tips to Drink More Water
•	Serve with Meals and Snacks: Offer water routinely at every meal and snack time rather than waiting for a child to ask.
•	Make it Accessible: Keep a refillable water bottle within easy reach at home, school, and during activities.
•	Model the Behavior: Children are more likely to choose water when they see parents and caregivers doing the same.
•	Add Natural Flavor: Infuse water with fruit slices (citrus, berries, cucumber) to make it more appealing.
8. During Illness
•	Diarrhea or Vomiting: Offer Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) in small, frequent sips to replace fluid and electrolyte losses.
•	Fever: Increase fluid offerings, as fever raises fluid loss through sweating and faster breathing.
•	Warning Sign: If a child cannot keep fluids down, shows signs of severe dehydration, or has reduced urination for over 8 hours, seek medical care.
9. Prevention of Dehydration
•	Offer Fluids Proactively: Don't wait for thirst — offer water regularly throughout the day, especially in hot weather or during activity.
•	Extra Fluids for Activity/Heat: Increase offerings before, during, and after physical activity or time outdoors in warm weather.
•	Monitor Urine Color: Pale yellow urine is a simple, reliable sign of good hydration in children old enough to use the toilet independently.
10. Fun Hydration Tip
•	The “Rainbow Water” Game: Let your child pick colorful, reusable straws or a favorite cup, and pair each glass of water with a water-rich fruit snack (like watermelon or orange slices) to make hydration a fun, everyday habit.
