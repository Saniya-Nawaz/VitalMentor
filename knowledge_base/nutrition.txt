Topic: Child Nutrition & Healthy Diet Summary
Based on the provided World Health Organization (WHO) fact sheet on a healthy diet, here are the detailed answers to your questions:
1. What is a balanced diet?
A balanced diet protects against malnutrition in all its forms (undernutrition, micronutrient deficiency, overweight, and obesity) as well as noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) such as diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and cancer. Its exact composition depends on individual characteristics (age, gender, lifestyle, level of physical activity), cultural context, locally available foods, and dietary customs, but the basic principles of what constitutes a healthy diet remain the same.
2. Essential nutrients
A healthy, balanced diet should provide energy and essential nutrients from diverse food sources, including:
Carbohydrates & Fiber: From whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables.
Proteins: From pulses (e.g., lentils, beans), nuts, and animal sources if consumed.
Fats: Un-saturated fats (found in fish, avocado, nuts, sunflower, soybean, canola, and olive oils).
Vitamins and Minerals: Micronutrients obtained primarily from fresh fruits, vegetables, and unrefined grains, along with potassium to counterbalance sodium intake.
3. Daily meal recommendations
Vegetables and Fruits: Eat at least 400 g (i.e., 5 portions) of fruit and vegetables per day (excluding potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava, and other starchy roots).
Grains and Pulses: Include whole grains (unprocessed maize, millet, oats, wheat, brown rice) and legumes (lentils, beans).
Fats: Total daily intake of fat should be less than 30% of total energy intake.
Saturated fats should account for less than 10% of total energy intake.
Trans-fats should account for less than 1% of total energy intake.
Free Sugars: Less than 10% of total energy intake (equivalent to 50 g or roughly 12 level teaspoons for a person of healthy body weight consuming ~2000 calories). A further reduction to less than 5% provides additional health benefits.
Salt/Sodium: Consume less than 5 g of salt per day (equivalent to about 1 teaspoon), ensuring it is iodized.
4. Healthy snacks
Healthy snacking options derived from the recommended food groups include:
Fresh, raw vegetables (e.g., sliced cucumbers, carrots).
Fresh fruits.
Unsalted nuts and seeds.
5. Foods to include
Fresh Fruits and Vegetables: A wide variety of colorful fruits and vegetables.
Legumes: Lentils, beans, and other pulses.
Whole Grains: Unprocessed maize, millet, oats, wheat, and brown rice.
Unsaturated Fats: Fish, avocado, nuts, sunflower, soybean, canola, and olive oils.
Iodized Salt: Essential for preventing iodine deficiency disorders.
6. Foods to limit
Free Sugars: Soft drinks, fruit juices/concentrates, flavored milks, sweet snacks, candies, and sugary syrups.
Saturated Fats: Fatty meat, butter, palm and coconut oil, cream, cheese, ghee, and lard.
Trans-Fats: Industrially-produced trans-fats found in baked, fried, pre-packaged foods, frozen pizza, pies, cookies, biscuits, and cooking oils/spreads.
Salt / High-Sodium Foods: Salty snacks, condiments (soy sauce, fish sauce, bouillon), processed meats, and high-sodium pre-packaged foods.
7. Water requirements
While the core fact sheet emphasizes limiting sugar-sweetened beverages in favor of natural hydration, proper fluid intake is crucial. Drinking clean, plain water is recommended to maintain hydration without adding extra calories or free sugars to the diet.
8. Common nutrition mistakes
Excessive Salt/Sodium Consumption: Consuming more than 5 g of salt daily, which contributes to high blood pressure and increased risk of heart disease and stroke.
High Intake of Free Sugars: Consuming sugary drinks and confectioneries leading to unhealthy weight gain, obesity, and dental caries (tooth decay).
Overconsumption of Saturated & Trans-Fats: Replacing healthy unsaturated fats with saturated and industrially produced trans-fats.
Inadequate Fruit and Vegetable Intake: Failing to meet the daily minimum of 400 g of fruits and vegetables.
9. Healthy eating habits
Include vegetables at every meal.
Eat fresh fruit and raw vegetables as snacks.
Eat fresh, seasonal fruits and vegetables.
Eat a variety of fruits and vegetables to ensure a well-rounded intake of micronutrients.
Reduce salt during meal prep: Limit high-sodium condiments (e.g., soy sauce, fish sauce) and avoid placing salt/salty condiments on the table.
Cook healthy: Steam or boil foods instead of frying them, and trim visible fat from meat.
10. Fun nutrition tip
"Eat the Rainbow!" Make your plate look like an artist's palette by mixing fruits and vegetables of different colors (reds, greens, oranges, purples). This ensures you get a wide mix of essential vitamins and minerals while keeping meals visually appealing and exciting!
